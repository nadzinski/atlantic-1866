Atlantic news lag

1 May – 30 September 1866 · days

When information travels by ship, the news lag follows a sawtooth pattern, with drops every time a ship with fresh news arrives from the other side of the ocean. There was also a brief period in late July and early August when Europe was linked to Newfoundland but news was still ferried by boat across the Gulf of St. Lawrence from Newfoundland to Nova Scotia (and thence to the United States). The broken cable across that stretch was fixed on 12 August, at which point news could flow from London to New York by telegraph alone.

    The Tyranny of Distance

    Atlantic 1866

    The year is 1866. It is summer. The steamship Great Eastern is laying the first durable telegraph cable link across the Atlantic. At the beginning of the summer, it takes about 12 days for information and news to cross between New York and London, because it must travel on a ship. By the end, it takes only hours to cross, and London news is in the New York papers within a day.

    The North Atlantic is a busy place in 1866. Steamships and sailing ships still plied the waves together. Steamers, with a more reliable schedule, carried the mail and much of the news. Both steam and sail carried passengers (especially emigrants from Europe to North America), and cargo: American cotton bound for Lancashire mills, tobacco, Canadian timber, gold from California, Pennsylvania oil, grain and flour. We've plotted the voyages of both steam and sail, based on a mass of data drawn from newspapers on both sides of the Atlantic.

    The summer of 1866 was rich in major news events, from a financial crisis in London, to the short Austro-Prussian War in Europe, to the passage of the 14th Amendment by the United States Congress. And the laying of the telegraph cable itself was followed eagerly by newspaper readers in Europe and America.
